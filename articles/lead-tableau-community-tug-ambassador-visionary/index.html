Lead a community: run a TUG and what Ambassadors and Visionaries ask

How to start or co-lead a Tableau User Group in 2026, how to run one that lasts (cadence, speakers, co-leads, recordings) and what the Tableau Ambassador and Visionary programmes actually ask of you.

12 Oct 2026 Tableau as of October 2026 Checked against Tableau Help Level 4 Ankur Napa CommunityPublic

Short answer

Community leadership is mostly logistics done kindly. A Tableau User Group needs at least two leaders, four useful events a year and a speaker pipeline that does not depend on one person's goodwill. Tableau supports leaders with a guidebook, reimbursement, swag and a dedicated community team, but the group belongs to its leaders. Ambassador and Visionary recognition follows a year of giving back; it is never the way in. If you are choosing where to start, co-lead an existing group before you found a new one.

The decision

Most people asking "how do I lead?" are choosing between four routes.

How a TUG leader is appointed

Leaders are not appointed by the group's members or by a vote. They apply to Tableau's TUG programme and the community team reviews the application. The same application covers starting a new group and joining an existing group's leadership team.

The 2026 rules, from the Start a Group page:

  • Application windows. Applications open in set windows. The current window runs from 1 to 31 October 2026, closing at 11:59 p.m. Pacific time. The previous one ran in July. Tableau's own guidance is that windows open roughly quarterly.
  • Product knowledge. At least six months with any Tableau product, shown on LinkedIn or Tableau Public, or a Credly profile if you are certified.
  • Attendance. You must have attended at least one TUG event before applying.
  • Slack. All leaders must be in the Tableau Community Slack workspace, where leader resources live.
  • Location. For a regional group, you live or work in that place.
  • Cadence. At least one event every three months, or four a year, that are useful, informative and engaging.
  • A co-leader. A new group needs one co-leader to apply with you. Incomplete co-leader submissions are not considered.
  • Restrictions. Salesforce and Tableau employees cannot lead a TUG. Leaders cannot all be from the same company. Students are not eligible at present. The group must not already exist in the same city; different types of group may share an area under some conditions.

To co-lead an existing group: talk to its current leaders first. Find them on the group's page under organisers, offer a specific job (speaker pipeline, venue, recordings, social posts) and agree it before you apply. Then submit the leader application in the open window, naming the group. The community team checks the details.

What Tableau gives leaders: swag for attendees, reimbursement, a dedicated community management team, the TUG Leader Guidebook, access to leader resources in Slack and discount codes for Tableau and Salesforce events.

Reference design

A user group that lasts looks less like a club and more like a small, well-run service.

ElementDesign
LeadershipTwo to four co-leads from different employers. Each owns one area: programme, venue, comms, recordings
CadenceQuarterly at minimum, as the programme asks. Monthly only if the speaker pipeline can carry it
Format mixAlternate virtual and in person. Hybrid only when someone owns the camera and the microphone
Session shapeOne main talk of 20 to 30 minutes, one short community slot, open questions
Speaker pipelineA standing call for speakers, the TUG speaker form, a list of three confirmed talks ahead
Newcomer pathA beginner-friendly session or show-and-tell every other event
RecordingsSpeaker consent agreed before the day, posted on the group page with slides and workbook links
CommsEvent page on usergroups.tableau.com, the #TableauUserGroup hashtag, a post after each event
SuccessionOne co-lead in training at all times, so the group survives someone changing jobs

For a working example of this pattern, the Bengaluru Tableau User Group keeps its events and recordings on its usergroups.tableau.com page and runs a separate community site with workshop pages, learning material and volunteer roles. It mixes virtual, in-person and hybrid sessions and invites volunteers to help plan. The lesson I take from running groups is the boring one: the event is easy, the calendar is the hard part.

What Ambassadors do, in Tableau's own description: answer questions in the community, publish content, lead and speak at user groups, teach Tableau APIs and share data viz best practice. In recent years they have also beta-tested products, mentored TUG leaders, helped select Viz of the Day and welcomed newcomers in the Tableau Community Slack. The Ambassador page lists each person with their Slack, Tableau Public, LinkedIn and Trailblazer profiles, so you can see what the role looks like in practice.

What Visionaries do: show a deep understanding of how Tableau works and invent new ways to use it, help others with correct and clear answers. They share tips that even Tableau's own developers had not considered. They also help Tableau build better products.

Export control rules mean Tableau cannot accept Ambassadors from some countries and regions. Applicants from some others may be accepted without benefits of monetary value. The application post lists them.

Questions to ask before you commit

  • Who is my co-leader? Do we work at different companies?
  • Can we name four event dates for the next twelve months today?
  • Who are our first three speakers? Who are the next three?
  • Where will we meet in person? Who pays for anything the reimbursement does not cover?
  • Who owns recordings? How do we get speaker consent?
  • What will this group do that the nearest existing group does not?
  • Who takes over if one of us changes job or city?

Review checklist

  • At least two leaders from different employers, both approved by the TUG programme
  • Next four events on the calendar, at least quarterly
  • A speaker list three deep, plus the speaker form link in every event post
  • A newcomer slot or beginner session in the rotation
  • Recording consent agreed with every speaker before the day
  • Slides and workbook links posted after each event
  • Code of Conduct linked on the group page and read out or shown at events
  • A named successor for each leadership role

Anti-patterns

  • The solo leader. It breaks the rules for new groups and it breaks the leader. Two is the minimum for a reason.
  • The vendor showcase. A group where every talk is a sales pitch for the speaker's employer empties fast. Mix practitioners, beginners and the occasional product session.
  • Monthly or nothing. A monthly cadence with no pipeline becomes three cancelled months. Quarterly done well beats monthly done badly.
  • Recording without asking. Speakers deserve a say. Some will present only if the recording is not published.
  • Collecting titles. Applying for Ambassador because you want the badge shows in the application. Tableau asks what you did for others over the year.
  • Founding a duplicate. If a group already covers your city, join it. The community team checks this. A split community is weaker for everyone.

On the brewhouse floor

A brewers' guild runs on the same mechanics as a TUG: a few volunteers, a calendar, a rotation of hosts and a newcomer who gets a mentor. A beverage-focused user group can bring brewers, distillers and winemakers who use Tableau into one room every quarter. Sessions could cover brewhouse yield, cask inventory valuation or harvest-to-bottle traceability, each built on public or invented data. Keep at least one co-lead who works on the floor rather than in an office, so the questions stay real. For session material, see an executive KPI scorecard for a brewery.
