Dates done right: date parts, date values and a real timeline

Tableau offers each date two ways: as a date part (blue, a label like July) or a date value (green, a point on a timeline). Pick the wrong one and the chart quietly adds two Julys together.

Short answer

Right-click a date pill and Tableau shows two groups of options. The upper group gives date parts: Year, Quarter, Month, Day. They are discrete, blue and become labels, so "January" means every January in the data added together. The lower group gives date values: the same names, but each one keeps the year. They are continuous, green and draw a timeline. Use date values for a trend. Use date parts to compare the same month across years, with the year on Color. And never compare a full year with a part year.

What you will build

Four small sheets for Harbour Goods, the fictional retailer used across this site, each showing one date choice and what it does to the answer:

  1. Sales by year and why it lies.
  2. Sales by month as a date part and why it also lies.
  3. Sales as a real monthly timeline.
  4. This year against last year, month by month, with a date filter.

The data is dates-in-tableau.csv: 148 clean orders from January 2025 to September 2026 across the six Harbour Goods stores. Same columns as the main sample, no messy rows, so the only surprises are the ones the dates cause.

Before you start

  • Tableau Desktop, any edition.
  • The Sales calculation from Your first workbook. You will create it again in step 1.
  • Two words. A date part is one piece of a date, like the month, with the rest thrown away. A date value (Tableau's help also calls it a date truncation) keeps the date down to a level, so "March 2026" stays different from "March 2025".

Steps

1. Connect and create Sales

  1. Select Connect > To a File > Text file and open dates-in-tableau.csv.
  2. On the data source page check that Order Date has a calendar icon.
  3. Open Sheet 1, select Analysis > Create Calculated Field, name it Sales and enter:
[Quantity] * [Unit Price] * (1 - [Discount])

2. Sales by year, the trap

  1. Name the sheet By year. Drag Order Date to Columns. It arrives as YEAR(Order Date), blue.
  2. Drag Sales to Rows.
  3. Two bars: 2025 is taller than 2026. A reader concludes the business shrank.

It did not. 2025 has twelve months and 2026 has nine. Tableau did exactly what you asked. Keep this sheet; it is the most common date mistake in real dashboards.

3. Sales by month as a date part, the second trap

  1. Create a new sheet called Month part. Drag Order Date to Columns.
  2. Right-click the pill and select Month in the upper group. The pill reads MONTH(Order Date) and stays blue.
  3. Drag Sales to Rows. Twelve bars, January to December.

January here is January 2025 plus January 2026. October to December hold only 2025. The bars compare different numbers of months. A date part is a label. Labels do not know what year they came from.

4. A real timeline

  1. Create a sheet called Timeline. Drag Order Date to Columns.
  2. Right-click the pill and select Month in the lower group. The example beside it shows a month with a year. The pill turns green.
  3. Drag Sales to Rows. Tableau draws one line with 21 points, January 2025 to September 2026, on a continuous axis.
  4. Right-click the date axis, select Format. On the Axis tab under Scale set the date format (the label may read Dates or Numbers) to a custom format such as mmm yy. The axis now reads Jan 25, Feb 25 and so on.

Now the October 2025 peak is visible and so is the dip in June 2026. That is what a trend chart is for.

5. This year against last year

  1. Create a sheet called Year on year. Drag Order Date to Columns, right-click it and pick Month from the upper group (blue, a date part).
  2. Drag Order Date again, this time onto Color on the Marks card. It arrives as YEAR(Order Date). Tableau gives each year its own colour.
  3. Drag Sales to Rows. Two lines, one per year, sharing a January to December axis.
  4. If the marks are bars, change the Marks card dropdown to Line.

Here the date part is right. You want every January together on one header, because the year is split out by colour.

6. Add a date filter

  1. Drag Order Date to the Filters shelf.
  2. The dialog asks how to filter. Select Range of Dates and then Next.
  3. Set the start to 1 October 2025 and the end to 30 September 2026. Select OK.
  4. Right-click the filter pill, select Apply to Worksheets > All Using This Data Source.

Every sheet now covers the same twelve months. The By year sheet becomes honest: twelve months, split across two calendar years.

Relative dates (the Relative dates option in the same dialog, such as Last 12 months) move with today's date. They suit a live dashboard and a published report. They make a walkthrough's numbers drift, so this one uses a fixed range.

The same file says "down" on sheet one and "up 22.7 per cent" on sheet four. Only the date choice changed.

Where this breaks

  • Dates stored as text. If Order Date shows Abc, Tableau sorts it alphabetically and cannot draw a timeline. Change the type on the data source page, or use DATEPARSE() in a calculation when the format is unusual.
  • Day and month swapped. A file written as 03/04/2026 can mean 3 April or 4 March. Tableau reads it with your computer's locale. Check one date you know before trusting any chart.
  • Missing months. A continuous axis draws a straight line across a month with no orders. A discrete one leaves a gap. Neither shows zero. Decide which story is true before you publish.
  • Fiscal years. If the business year starts in April, right-click Order Date in the Data pane, select Default Properties > Fiscal Year Start and pick April. Otherwise every year total is wrong for finance.
  • Week numbers. The day a week starts on follows your locale unless you set it. If you report ISO weeks, set the week start and calendar in the data source's date properties (Data > your data source > Date Properties).

On the certification

This maps to the Tableau Desktop Foundations exam guide objectives 4.2.3 (explain the difference between discrete date parts and continuous date values), 2.2.5 (add a date filter), 2.1.2 (line chart) and 1.3.5 (change default properties such as date format). Expect at least one question that shows a blue and a green date pill and asks which chart each draws.

On the brewhouse floor

A brewery's monthly volume chart falls into the same trap every January. The year-to-date bar for the new year looks tiny beside last year's full one. Someone asks why sales collapsed. Put the brew month on Columns as a date part and the year on Color. January then sits next to January. For the fermentation curve, use continuous day values, because day three of a ferment in March and day three in April are not the same moment in the tank.
